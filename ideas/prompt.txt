## Research workflow

1. Start from the live repository. Read `README.md`, `purpose.md`, `schema.md`, relevant source/concept/synthesis pages, and the corresponding `raw/sources/...` parses.
2. Use local search, graph links, repository tools, and any available research connectors as appropriate. Read primary-source parses for the papers that support the final idea. Do not rely on titles or snippets alone.
3. Map the main research clusters, recurring system models, decision variables, objectives, constraints, assumptions, limitations, and stated future work.
4. Generate at least three substantially different candidate ideas internally. For each candidate, test:
   - the concrete operational problem and why it matters;
   - the closest existing work and the remaining technical problem;
   - whether the novelty is a real change in state, lifecycle, resource relationship, timescale, uncertainty, safety, reliability, or service continuity;
   - whether the idea is more than replacing one optimizer or DRL algorithm with another;
   - whether the system model, objective, variables, constraints, and method can be closed;
   - whether executable baselines and falsifiable hypotheses exist.
5. Adversarially test the strongest candidates. Search for papers that could invalidate each novelty claim, subtract overlapping objects, states, variables, objectives, constraints, and methods, then revise or reject weak candidates. Do not infer global absence from a failed search. Use bounded wording such as “在本知识库及本轮检索范围内未发现直接覆盖该组合的工作，仍需外部系统性检索确认”。
6. For at least five critical papers, verify the curated page against its raw parse. Locate system assumptions, problem statements, objective functions, constraints, limitations, future work, and algorithm steps with `rg` or equivalent repository inspection.
7. Derive the algorithm from the problem structure. Explain which parts admit analytical solutions, decomposition, relaxation, robustification, or timescale separation, and which parts genuinely require online learning or optimization.
8. Continue through retrieval, falsification, revision, and consistency checks until one complete idea is ready. Do not stop at a plan or a candidate list.

Use bounded, non-overlapping subagents when they materially improve coverage. Suitable roles include evidence mapping, novelty adversarial review, and model/algorithm auditing. The lead agent remains responsible for the final judgment, primary-source checks, and final file.

## Idea quality requirements

The main contribution must come from a meaningful operational tension, such as:

- a new task or service lifecycle;
- persistent energy or battery constraints;
- service continuity or state handover;
- mobility, uncertainty, safety, or reliability;
- a new coupling between computation, communication, trajectory, storage, or control timescales;
- a realistic information limitation or partial-observability condition.

The following are insufficient by themselves:

- replacing DQN, PPO, SAC, MAPPO, or another algorithm;
- adding an arbitrary weighted objective to a familiar trajectory/offloading problem;
- stacking RIS, NOMA, ISAC, digital twins, blockchain, LLMs, or similar labels without operational necessity;
- claiming generic performance improvement without a new falsifiable tension;
- tuning parameters in one static simulation setting.

For every important assumption, state why it is needed, what it simplifies, how it differs from reality, and how it could later be relaxed or validated.

## Model closure checklist

Before finalizing the document, verify that:

1. Every object, set, index, timeslot, and unit is defined before first use.
2. Task arrivals, input size, computational intensity, state, and deadlines are defined where relevant.
3. Communication rates and channel assumptions match the proposed scenario.
4. Local, UAV, and other edge computation processes are defined where relevant.
5. Propulsion, transmission, computation, and migration energy are not double-counted.
6. Delay, queue, reliability, and service-continuity quantities have consistent definitions.
7. Every decision variable appears in the objective or a constraint.
8. Every objective term is computable from defined variables and states.
9. The constraint set is not obviously contradictory.
10. At least one feasible baseline policy can be constructed.
11. For stochastic, robust, online, or partially observable models, uncertainty and available information are explicit.

## Algorithm design checklist

Explain:

- the formal problem class and the couplings that make it difficult;
- which parts admit analytical treatment, decomposition, relaxation, approximation, or robust optimization;
- which parts genuinely require online learning or adaptive control;
- how discrete and continuous decisions are handled;
- how feasibility is enforced beyond reward penalties;
- where training data or online interactions come from;
- where training and online execution occur;
- what convergence, complexity, approximation, or performance analysis is realistic;
- why the proposed method is preferable to both pure optimization and end-to-end DRL for this problem.
